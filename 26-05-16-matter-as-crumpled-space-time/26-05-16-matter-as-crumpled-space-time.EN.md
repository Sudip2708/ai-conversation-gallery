# 1. The Mass of an Electron in Space

## Question

A fun question occurred to me: If an electron is "massless," how many of them would need to be in a small space for that object to have a hypothetically measurable mass?

## Answer

Hey there! :-)
This is actually a really interesting question because the electron is a bit of a trickster—it’s often said to be "massless" or almost massless, but in reality, it does have a rest mass. It’s just incredibly tiny.

The mass of a single electron is approximately:

[
m_e \approx 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}
]

That means:
* 0.000000000000000000000000000000911 kg
Which is an absurdly small number! 😄

### How Many Electrons Would Make 1 Gram?

1 gram = 0.001 kg

So:

[
\frac{0.001}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}} \approx 1.1 \times 10^{27}
]

That’s approximately:
### ≈ 1,100,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 electrons
That’s roughly an **octillion** electrons.

### But Here’s the Catch—Electrons Repel Each Other

This is a critical issue:
Electrons have a negative charge, so if you tried to pack them into a small space, they would violently repel each other due to the electromagnetic force.
And by the way, this force is *much* stronger than gravity.

So:
* Theoretically, mass would arise even from a small number of electrons,
* But creating a "ball of electrons" is almost impossible,
* Because they would instantly fly apart.

### For a "Measurable" Mass

This depends on the sensitivity of the measurement.

For example:

Mass and Required Number of Electrons

| Mass         | Required Number of Electrons |
|--------------|-----------------------------:|
| 1 kg         |       \(1.1 \times 10^{30}\) |
| 1 g          |       \(1.1 \times 10^{27}\) |
| 1 mg         |       \(1.1 \times 10^{24}\) |
| 1 microgram  |       \(1.1 \times 10^{21}\) |

Even a microgram is an unimaginable number of electrons.

### Fun Fact

In reality, almost all the mass of ordinary matter **does not come from electrons**.
Electrons are light.
Most of an atom’s mass comes from protons and neutrons—and even there, the mass doesn’t mainly come from the "mass of quarks," but from the energy of their motion and binding inside the nucleus, according to:

[
E = mc^2
]

So, a large part of "matter" is actually trapped energy.
This is one of those moments where physics starts to get truly mind-blowing! 😄
